Practical quantum key distribution (QKD) is secure only while its devices match the assumptions of the security proof, and implementation attacks exploit mismatches while keeping the quantum bit error rate (QBER) low. We formulate attack detection in decoy-state BB84 as a sequential test against the honest-device manifold, the set of receiver statistics an attack-free link can produce under arbitrary channel loss. The resulting monitor, Q-TRACE (Quantum Telemetry Residuals Against Calibrated Expectations), calibrates a physical model of each link on commissioning data, profiles out the transmittance, and combines omnibus residual, physics score and setting-allocation tests with drift-robust references; no attack data are used. The formulation also yields a detectability exponent D* that lower-bounds the delay of any telemetry monitor and vanishes for a Trojan-horse probe with filtered back-reflection. On a count-level twin with model mismatch, over five held-out seeds and at a per-window false-alarm rate of 10^-3, Q-TRACE detects 63.6% of links under known attacks, 28.0% under unseen attack families and 65.3% under composite attacks, versus at most 51.2%, 6.7% and 42.3% for industry monitors, unsupervised and quantum-kernel detectors and a supervised classifier. Its delay attains the bound for strongly detectable attacks. On 33 parallel BB84 links of the IBM Heron processor ibm_marrakesh with physically executed entangling and amplitude-damping eavesdroppers, the measured information-disturbance relations agree with theory; a commissioning reference acquired under different operating conditions triggers alarms in every monitor tested, while a condition-matched reference detects a probe with 0.3% ideal QBER increase on 14 of 14 link-epochs with no false alarm on 66 honest ones.
