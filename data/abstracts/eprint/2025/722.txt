Threshold cryptographic primitives have not been widely adopted beyond the closed committee model, presumably due to state-synchronization overhead and complex certification processes for the shareholders. These are both aspects of their overreliance on infrastructure, an assumption that is usually glossed over in their design. Certifying individual identities is at odds with proactive sharing, just as interacting upon every session conflicts with open or cross-domain environments; indeed, in such settings, distributed identity is more naturally modelled as a fluid group of shareholders that need not interact continuously and can reshare occasionally without need for external entities to take notice. In this work, we investigate a plausible approach to threshold identification that achieves non-interactivity and non-reliance on advertised shares by having the provers attach them to ordinary Schnorr proofs on the fly. Given a Shamir $(n, t)$-shared secret $x$, the resulting protocol allows any $t^* \ge t$ (but no less) shareholders to prove over designated communication channels that their keys interpolate to $x$. Provers do not engage in multi-party computations, sending their packets to the verifier asynchronously; conversely, verifiers need only know the combined public key $y \equiv g ^ x$, without need to pre-validate and register the individual member identities. The protocol is intended for use in permissionless or unmanaged meshes that lack both overlay networks and trust infrastructure or governance, a use case space that has received little attention. No verifiable setup is required beyond distributing $x$ according to Shamir's sharing (or equivalent distributed key generation scheme) and advertising its public counterpart; in particular, the protocol is intended to be secure against impersonation attacks without relying on the auditability of any advertised shares. We provide evidence that this has good chances to hold true by giving a formal security proof in the random oracle model under the discrete-logarithm (\textsf{DL}) hardness assumption.
