Verifiable Secret Sharing (VSS) ensures consistent sharing and correct reconstruction, but does not identify the shareholders involved. Traceable Secret Sharing (TSS) expands secret sharing with two guarantees: $\textit{traceability}$, which identifies the makers of a reconstruction box through black-box queries; and $\textit{non-imputability}$, which prevents false accusations against honest shareholders. However, full traceability is practically expensive: existing Shamir-based schemes require multiple carefully chosen queries to a $\textit{stateless}$ reconstruction box. Moreover, Goyal et al. (ePrint 2025/1980) show that the strongest form of black-box tracing is incompatible with simultaneously achieving non-imputability and tracer secrecy. We introduce $\textit{Accountable VSS}$ (Acc-VSS), a practical extension of VSS that achieves the next-best goal: $\textit{accountability}$, a strict relaxation of traceability requiring that (i) the contributors to every accepted reconstruction be publicly identifiable from its transcript with a $\textit{single}$ query, and (ii) no honest shareholder be falsely accused, even by an adversary holding all other shares. Accountability thus replaces black-box, multi-query tracing with a transcript check executable by any external verifier or smart-contract judge. In this paper, (i) we give a generic transformation from any computationally secure Shamir-based VSS to Acc-VSS, adding only one field element per share; it uses a long-term identity secret to each share and a conjunctive NIZK to bind partial reconstructions to the shareholder and the dealer's commitments; (ii) we instantiate it with Feldman and Pedersen VSS and prove the security of both schemes; (iii) we prove a self-contained $\textit{single-query}$ impossibility lemma motivating our relaxation; and (iv) we show how Acc-VSS uniformly yields accountable threshold ElGamal decryption, Schnorr/FROST signing, and distributed key generation. For the benchmarked VSS-based threshold-decryption protocol, accountability adds at most $20.3\%$ end-to-end overhead for $n=20$ and less than $6\%$ for $n=100$. Encrypted mempools, end-to-end-verifiable e-voting tallies, and validator-slashing in proof-of-stake systems all reduce to this framework.
